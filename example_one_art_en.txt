The latest exhibition by sculptor Iris Thorne, titled "Concrete Botany," is a profound and unsettling exploration of the tension between the natural world and the urban environment. Housed in a raw, industrial warehouse space, the collection immediately confronts the visitor with its stark materiality. Thorne’s work has always been characterized by a powerful juxtaposition of organic forms and man-made materials, but here, this theme reaches its most poignant and sophisticated expression. The exhibition functions as a sculptural ecosystem, where life and decay are locked in a silent, unending struggle. It challenges our romanticized notions of nature, presenting it not as a pristine ideal but as a resilient, and sometimes monstrous, force adapting to a hostile world.
Thorne’s primary materials are concrete, rusted rebar, and polished bronze, which she uses to construct her hybrid forms. In pieces like "Urban Bloom," a delicate, flower-like structure meticulously crafted from polished bronze appears to forcefully erupt from a cracked slab of rough concrete. The beauty of the metallic blossom is inseparable from the violence of its birth. Another powerful work, "Root System," features a web of twisted rebar that mimics the intricate network of roots, spreading across the gallery floor and seemingly breaking through the concrete foundation itself. The use of scale is masterful; some pieces are small and intimate, inviting close inspection, while others are monumental, dominating the space and dwarfing the viewer. This variation creates a dynamic rhythm throughout the exhibition.
The conceptual framework of "Concrete Botany" delves into themes of resilience, adaptation, and ecological anxiety. Thorne is not merely placing nature and industry side by side; she is fusing them into singular, inseparable entities. Her sculptures can be read as speculative artifacts from a post-human future, questioning what forms life might take after our era. There is a melancholic beauty to these objects, a sense of deep time embodied in the slow corrosion of metal and the unyielding hardness of concrete. They are monuments to a world where the boundaries between the artificial and the natural have completely dissolved. The artist forces us to consider the legacy of our built environment and its permanent entanglement with the biosphere.
While the exhibition is conceptually rigorous and visually stunning, it occasionally leans too heavily on its core aesthetic. After viewing a dozen similar pieces, the initial shock of the material contrast begins to wane, and a certain predictability sets in. The smaller works, in particular, sometimes feel like mere studies for the larger, more ambitious installations, lacking their commanding presence. A greater diversity in form or a more explicit narrative thread in some sections might have enhanced the overall impact. Nevertheless, "Concrete Botany" is a triumph of contemporary sculpture. Iris Thorne has created a powerful, thought-provoking body of work that is both formally elegant and thematically urgent. It is an exhibition that lingers in the mind long after one has left the gallery, leaving behind a haunting vision of a world remade in concrete and steel.
